# Why do the blades rotate?

The basic principles governing the forces that appear on the wind turbine blades and force the Hub, to which they are connected, to rotate, are the same ones that allow airplanes to ascend. The rotation is due to the Hub having only one degree of freedom: rotating on its axis.

## What are these forces?

Let us suppose we have an *airplane wing* in a wind tunnel, as shown in Fig 1. In a wind tunnel the air flow is uniform, so that there are no turbulences (that is, "*laminar flow*"). And we can vary its intensity at will.

Inside this wind tunnel the wing is placed (represented by its section, delimited by the red line), and we fix it well to the outside of the wind tunnel, so that it cannot move. This fixing is done with an *internal bar* that crosses the wing along its longest axis (the circle is shown in the center), and allows us to rotate. In this way we can change the angle that the wing chord presents to the wind.

![](<lib/Bladeconfuerzas.png>)

Fig 1.

The effect of the wind, which comes from the left, on the wing is a *flow compression* on the lower side of the blade, and a depression on its upper side. The result is a set of forces applied along the blade surface: this set of forces is indicated by the small dotted arrows.

Since the blade is a *rigid body*, the total effect of these forces can be assimilated to a single force whose value is the vector sum of all of them, and that is applied at the fixing point, the bar represented by the circle: this force is indicated by the **red vector**. The magnitude and direction of this force depends on:

- the *blade shape*,

- the incoming *wind speed*,

- the *angle* between the *blade chord* (**gray line** joining the two blade ends) and the wind direction (**arrow** on the left).

We can decompose this red vector at will: in this particular case, it is convenient to think of this red vector as the sum of two forces: one to go up, called ***Flifting***, and another that goes to the right, named ***Fdragging***.

These two forces explain why the airplane is kept hanging in the air (due to *Flifting*), and why it must be pushed to the left (to overcome Fdragging), so that the airplane remains suspended in the air. This force is the one that the airplane engines must overcome.

Now let us change the airplane wing for the blade of a wind turbine and place it in relation to its axis. The situation is similar, but inverted. See the following figure where the vision that an observer located at the tip of one of the blades and looking at the Hub axis would have is presented. The wind would be facing them and the blades would rotate to their left in a plane perpendicular to the screen and passing through the **black vector**.

![](<lib/Hub con vientos y fuerzas - espaol.png>)

Fig 2.

In this case, the wind incident on the blade edge is the "Total Wind" (**red vector**), a composition of the incident wind (**blue vector**) and the *rotational* wind (**black vector**), which is the one produced by the blade when it rotates, because it is at a certain distance from the rotation point (the Hub axis). We are assuming that the rotation occurs "to the left" and due to that movement the blunter edge of the blade "*cuts perpendicularly*" the incident wind. The speed of that cut is not uniform along that edge: lower the closer the blade section we are seeing is to the Hub center (see [Blades](EsquemafuncionaldeunaAerogenerad.md)). This air cutting speed is equivalent to a wind that had an opposite direction and the blade remained static.

In this brief explanation we will assume that the speed of this wind that interests us is the "average" along the entire edge.

The left representation is a way of solving the problem of representing the rotating blade, by making the change of proposing a "view from the blade itself", as if we were sitting at the end of the blade looking towards the rotation axis in the Hub. The black vector would correspond to the wind we would feel on the face due to that rotation.

The total force is represented by the **green vector**, and similarly to the wind tunnel and airplane wing case, we decompose it into two forces: one that pulls the blade along the only degree of freedom it has (the rotation plane), and another (**light brown vector**) goes in the direction of the Hub axis, and is counteracted by the Tower; that is, this is the force that tries to bend the Tower.

This force set depends on the relative angle of the blade with respect to its rotation plane: that is, in the previous figure, on the angle between the **red dotted line** and the horizontal axis.
