# Introduction to induction machines

Electric induction generators (really electrical induction *machines*, since they can be *motors* or *generators*) are characterized by a curve that shows the relationship between the **mechanical torque** on their rotation shaft, and the **rotation speed of this shaft**. See the following figure.

![](<lib/MaquinaInduccionParvsRPMs2Puntos.jpg>)

Fig 1: "Mechanical torque on the shaft as a function of the shaft rotation speed" curve of an asynchronous induction machine.

There is a characteristic point of this graph, defined by the **synchronous speed**. This speed corresponds to the *rotation speed of the magnetic flux* created in its air gap by the stator winding, when it is connected to an Electrical Grid, and when <u>there is neither mechanical load (nothing braking it), nor mechanical torque contribution (it is not connected to any motor) on its shaft</u>.

To understand the two operating modes, motor or generator, we must remember that this device transforms energy from one type to another (electrical energy into mechanical energy, or mechanical energy into electrical energy). Therefore it "connects" two worlds:

- the **rotation shaft**, where the rotor winding is located, which must be mechanically connected to a device that can turn, and which can behave as a mechanical load or as a mechanical energy generator.

- the **stator winding**, which must be connected to an electrical energy *source* or to an electrical energy *consumer*.

The previous graph considers the shaft rotation speed, but says nothing about how the shaft is brought to that speed. To understand the physical principles on which this machine is based, we can begin by visualizing the following video "[Facing magnets on shafts](https://youtu.be/pvqHvcnltEk)". The idea intended to be shown in it, is that the *operating principle of electromechanical machines* is based on having *two magnetic fluxes* (equivalent to magnets), located very close to each other, so that they interact with each other. Each of these magnets is attached to two non-deformable cylinders, which can rotate on independent but coaxial shafts (the directions of both shafts are a single one).

One of the shafts (the left one) has a device that allows controlling the friction of the left cylinder with its support (even its stop), and therefore is equivalent to a <u>mechanical load</u>. On the other shaft we have a mechanism (*crank*) with which we can create a <u>mechanical energy source</u>. *The mission of the two magnets is to transmit the (mechanical) energy* from the source on the right to the mechanical load located on the left. What this video wants to emphasize is that the magnets are *intermediary* elements, that is, the energy transferred from one cylinder to the other does not come out of or enter the magnets.

In the case shown in the video, where the left cylinder is fixed, the independent variables handled are:

- the energy transmitted (the torque applied to the crank),

- the magnetic force of the magnets (their magnetic flux), and

- the distance between them (**air gap**).

The *dependent variable* is the *angle between the magnet axes*. This angle is greater the greater the energy applied to the crank and the friction on the left shaft, and is inversely proportional to the magnet flux: with infinitely "powerful" magnets this angle would always be 0. In the case that the left cylinder is not fixed, it will have a minimum friction torque, so that if the energy contributed by the right cylinder is lower than that friction, we will be in the same situation described above. If it is greater, the whole system will rotate with a speed that will depend on the energy excess and on the set moment of inertia. That is, we will have the cylinders *rotating at different speeds*, there will be a **slip** of the right magnet rotation speed over the left one speed. This concept is crucial to understand these machines.

For this example to serve to explain asynchronous machines we need to know <u>*how to create the two magnets*</u> of the previous video.

## Magnetic field in the air gap generated by the stator

In the following animated graph the stator winding of a three-phase asynchronous machine is shown, in a simplified way, prepared to be connected to a three-phase alternating current Electrical Grid. Unlike a single-phase grid, in the three-phase one we have three generators synchronous with each other (that is, they operate at the same frequency), which have a separation between phases of 120 electrical degrees (right panel of the graph).

![](<lib/CompletoConCamposDeFaseImanVirtual_2.gif>)

Fig. 2

Each grid phase is connected to a stator coil (one per color). The *magnet* (that is, the magnetic flux) presented, corresponds to the *vector sum* of the magnetic fluxes created by each of the coils, fed by each of the grid phases. Each flux is identified with the color assigned to the corresponding coil pair.

This is one of the magnets needed in the previous video, with the particularity that this *magnet is not fixed, but rotates around the central axis of the machine* (which is not shown). This aspect is fundamental for this type of machine.

If we now place a winding on the shaft and short-circuit it, as shown in the following image, the situation will be *almost similar* to having a transformer with the secondary short-circuited, and crossed by the magnetic field (virtual magnet); the difference with the transformer is that, in this case, the secondary (rotor winding) *is moving*.

![](<lib/Maquina jaula de ardilla montada.jpg>)

Faraday's law tells us that, when that occurs, a voltage is induced in the rotor winding (secondary of the equivalent transformer), whose frequency will be the one corresponding to the *relative* movement of the rotating magnet and the rotor movement. If we short-circuit the rotor, that voltage will create a current in it. That current induced in the rotor creates the *second magnet* in our previous video.

If we keep the rotor fixed (through a brake), then the relative movement of the magnet will correspond to the frequency of the electrical grid that feeds the stator (primary of the transformer): the result is that the current induced in the rotor will create the second magnet. Exactly as if we had a transformer with the secondary short-circuited.

However, if the rotor is free to rotate, any small non-uniformity in the flux of the magnet crossing it (due to deformations, or irregularities, etc.) will translate into a small angular displacement of the rotor, initially at a small speed. As a consequence, the rotation speed of the stator magnet, *seen by the rotor winding*, will be lower than before. This rotation speed difference will keep increasing until the (physical) rotation speed of the rotor shaft is equal to the rotation speed of the stator magnet. When that happens, there will be no induction in the rotor winding, since the stator magnet flux will not cross the rotor winding, because they will be rotating at the same speed, *synchronously*. That is the **synchronous speed**.

Let us see what happens if some external system interacts with our machine: it can only be through the rotor shaft.

## Case with mechanical load on the rotor shaft

If initially our machine is at synchronous speed, and we place a load (a brake) on the shaft, it will reduce its rotation speed. As a consequence, the rotor winding will be crossed by a magnetic flux corresponding to a speed equal to that of the stator magnet *minus* the shaft rotation speed. Initially it will be the synchronism one, and it will progressively decrease. Down to what speed will it drop? The only parameter we have is the *resisting mechanical torque* (see point A in figure 1). That is, when the shaft rotation speed is below synchronism, an induced current will appear that will create the second magnet, so that the magnet positioning shown in the video "[Facing magnets on shafts](https://youtu.be/pvqHvcnltEk)" occurs. That angle between the magnets will depend on the stator flux magnitude (how strong the stator magnet is), on the air gap between the magnets, and on the magnitude of the flux created by the current induced in the rotor by the stator.

In figure 1 (above) it is shown that when a **friction** is introduced on the shaft, its rotation speed decreases and the machine ***creates a mechanical torque*** that counteracts the friction. (Point A.)

Practical example: if we have a drill with a bit separated from the wall, the shaft rotation speed will be the synchronism one, because there is no mechanical load. When we press the drill + bit against the wall, the bit will create a resisting torque, and the shaft rotation speed will decrease. This will be noticed by the sound emitted by the drill in both cases.

## Case with mechanical torque applied to the rotor shaft

On the other hand, if the shaft is **forced** to go at a speed higher than the synchronism one, it ***consumes the mechanical torque*** that is necessary for that forcing; remember that the dimension equation of "mechanical torque" = "force x distance to the rotation axis", is identical to that of energy (Work) = "force x distance traveled". That contributed mechanical energy appears as electrical energy in the stator coils. (Point B in figure 1.) This can be achieved by coupling the blades of a wind turbine to the shaft.

That is, depending on whether we place a mechanical load on the shaft or supply mechanical torque, this machine behaves as a motor or as a generator.

## About the shaft rotation direction

An asynchronous induction machine can act as a motor or as a generator, depending on its shaft rotation speed: the rotation direction is the same in both cases.

## Torque as a function of the slip

In figure 1 it can be observed that the zone around the synchronous speed has an almost linear section, before it reaches a point of maximum or minimum torque.

If the shaft is in that zone (the "S" one) then the mechanical torque consumed or generated is proportional to the slip, whose formula is:

*s = (RealSpeed - SynchronousSpeed) / SynchronousSpeed*

or

*s = (RealSpeed / SynchronousSpeed) - 1*

The **synchronous speed** depends on the number of 'magnetic pole pairs' (1 in the case of the upper figure) and on the grid frequency. In the case of the figure, and assuming 50 cycles/second, the synchronous speed is (50 cycles/second)/1, that is 50 revolutions/second, or (50 * 60) revolutions/minute = (3,000 rpms).
