# Electrical Grid concepts

The Electrical Grid that reaches the *Wind Farms* has the following characteristics:

- it is integrated into the **high voltage grid**

- with a three-phase delta line (without neutral)

- with a voltage range above 100 kV.

- controlled by the "Transmission System Operator" (**TSO**). In Spain the company Red Eléctrica de España.

- the wind turbines of a Wind Farm do NOT connect to the Electrical Grid directly, but through a (single) electrical Substation, at a voltage level called **Medium Voltage** (below 32 kV).

- The wind turbine electric generators usually operate at 690 V between phases, a range considered **Low Voltage**. To reduce the ohmic losses (through cable heating), each wind turbine has a step-up transformer (from 690 V to 32 kV). This transformer can be inside the tower, or in a small building near the wind turbine.

- The output cables of this transformer connect to the Medium Voltage part of the Substation. These connections are NOT radial, but there are cable sections shared by several wind turbines.

- The Substation has elements for the connection and disconnection of the Farm, as a whole, from the high voltage line.

- The Substation has cutting and connection elements with each of the cable groups that connect with the wind turbines.

Other aspects to remember:

⚡ Electrical Grid Frequency
- It is NOT fixed at 50 Hz.  
The system frequency continuously oscillates around its nominal value because it reflects the instantaneous balance between generation and demand.

- It is NOT identical at all points of the grid.  
Small spatial variations appear due to transmission impedances, load dynamics, and the propagation of disturbances across the network.

🔌 Voltage at the Wind Farm Point of Connection
The voltage at the farm’s output terminals must remain within a narrow, predefined range established by the grid code.
Operating outside this band may trigger protection systems or violate the farm’s connection agreement.

⚙️ Reactive Power Requirements
The TSO (Transmission System Operator) may require the wind farm to deliver a specific level of reactive power, either capacitive or inductive, to support:

- voltage regulation,

- grid stability,

- dynamic response during disturbances.

These requirements are defined in the grid code and may vary depending on operating conditions.

In an electrical grid, the frequency is often described as “50 Hz,” but this value is only nominal. In reality, the frequency is constantly moving, rising and falling slightly as the system responds to the instantaneous balance between generation and consumption. It is a living signal, not a fixed number. And because the grid is a vast, interconnected physical system, this frequency is not perfectly identical everywhere: small variations appear from one point to another as power flows through transmission lines, encounters impedance, and reacts to the dynamic behavior of loads and generators.

At the wind farm’s point of connection, voltage must remain within a narrow and clearly defined band. This requirement is not arbitrary; it ensures that the farm operates safely, that its equipment is protected, and that it does not disturb the stability of the surrounding network. Deviations outside this range can trigger protections or violate the conditions under which the farm is allowed to inject power into the grid.

Beyond active power, the Transmission System Operator may also require the wind farm to provide a specific level of reactive power. This is part of the farm’s responsibility as a grid‑connected generator: supporting voltage control, contributing to system stability, and helping the network respond to disturbances. Depending on the operating conditions, the TSO may ask the farm to behave as a source or sink of reactive power, ensuring that the grid remains balanced and resilient.

⚡ Continuación narrativa
Just as frequency and voltage reveal the pulse of the electrical system, the behavior of a wind farm must adapt continuously to the conditions imposed by the grid. A farm is not an isolated machine; it is a participant in a much larger organism, one that expects every generator to contribute to stability rather than disturb it.

When the grid experiences a disturbance—perhaps a sudden change in load, a fault in a distant transmission line, or a large generator tripping offline—the wind farm must respond in a way that supports the system. This may involve adjusting active power output, modifying reactive power injection, or following predefined fault‑ride‑through profiles that allow the farm to remain connected even when voltage dips or oscillates. These requirements are not optional; they are part of the grid code that defines how modern renewable plants coexist with the rest of the electrical infrastructure.

At the same time, the farm’s internal control systems must coordinate hundreds of individual turbines, each reacting to wind conditions, mechanical constraints, and electrical commands. The farm controller acts as the conductor of this orchestra, ensuring that the combined output behaves as a single, predictable generator from the grid’s perspective. It smooths fluctuations, enforces limits, and negotiates with the TSO’s demands so that the farm remains compliant while extracting as much energy as possible from the wind.

In essence, connecting a wind farm to the grid is not merely a matter of producing power. It is about behaving correctly within a complex, dynamic environment—one where frequency drifts, voltage must be respected, and reactive power becomes a tool for maintaining balance. The farm must listen to the grid, understand what it needs, and respond with precision. Only then does it become a reliable contributor to the electrical system rather than a source of instability.
