# Blades

The wind turbine blades have requirements similar to the airplane wings. In [Why do the blades rotate?](PFAPorquegiranlaspalas.md) the physical principles that make the wind turbine blades rotate are explained. The force on a blade can be decomposed into two forces: one similar to the one that keeps the airplane in the air (lift force or lifting in English), which in the wind turbine are the ones that make the blades rotate; and another force (dragging) opposes the air flow and has to be compensated by the airplane engine thrust, or in the case of wind turbines, must be supported by the Tower, which it tries to bend.

The relationship between these two forces (*lifting* and *dragging*) depends on the blade section shape (***air foil*** in English). In the following figure several types are shown. Logically, designs that maximize the lift/drag ratio are sought. In general, the best lift/drag ratios are had when the thickness-to-chord ratio (distance between air entry and exit edge) is in the order of 10% to 15%.

An important difference between the working conditions of wind turbine blades and airplane wings is that the wind that an airplane wing "sees" is uniform, while the one a wind turbine blade "sees" is not.

The following figure (3) shows the two wind sources that reach the blade. On one hand there is the "real" wind incident on the blade rotation plane, indicated by the **Win** vector, which is uniform along the blade, and parallel to the ground.

The other incident wind on the blades (**Wrot**) is due to their rotation: this wind is proportional to the rotation speed that the blades have at a given moment, and depends on where it is to be measured along the blade: the farther from the rotation axis, the more wind.

**|Wrot| = 2 \*** **π** **\* DistanceFromSectionToHubCenter \* Wind**

The direction of this rotation wind is a vector that is in the blade rotation plane, and therefore is perpendicular to the real wind (**Win**). The resulting wind (**Wt**) is the vector composition of both winds.
