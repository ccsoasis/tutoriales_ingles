# Evolution of the Magnetic Fluxes in the Generator and of the B2B signals

!!! tip "🎯 Evolution of the magnetic fluxes and B2B signals"
    ***Objective:*** Understand the RSC and GSC activation sequence during cutin and observe the evolution of the generator magnetic fluxes in subsynchronous mode.
    ***What to observe:*** The rotor flux direction (green vector) with respect to the mechanical rotation, the moment of stator connection, and the angular difference between rotor and stator flux in production.
    💡 In subsynchronous mode the rotor flux vector rotates in the same direction as the mechanical shaft. Why?

!!! warning "❓ Guiding questions"
    1. At what moment does the RSC connect to the generator rotor? What condition must be met before?
    2. What is the angular difference between the rotor flux and the stator flux when the system is in stable production with 7 m/s? And with 9 m/s? What is the difference due to?
    3. What happens to the generator magnetic flux in steps 1 and 2 (before connecting the RSC)? Why?

Repeat the practice [Evolution in the Power vs rpms plane](CPArranqueVientoMenorSincPotRPMs.md) but looking at the "*Doubly-fed induction generator with Back-to-Back converter*" synoptic.

## Evolution

1 - When the Controller receives the START order it makes sure that the GSC converter is effectively connected to the grid and that there is energy in the DC Link (capacitor), and that the RSC converter is inactive and disconnected from the Generator Rotor. At this moment there is a small grid consumption due to the losses in the GSC itself, transformer and DC Link.

![](<lib/B2B inicio conexion GSC.jpg>)

2 - When the brake is released, the RSC converter becomes activated, although it is still disconnected from the Generator Rotor.

![](<lib/B2B comienzo RSC alimentado pero no conectado.jpg>)

3 - When the Power Train reaches a speed such that the signals to be supplied to the Generator Rotor are within the RSC operating range (typically around 25% of the Generator synchronous speed), then the RSC is connected, which goes on to supply a voltage such that the total flux in the air gap generated by the Rotor (red vector inside the Rotor winding) has an angular speed equal to the one the stator-generated flux will have when it connects to the grid. There is really no power transfer, since the stator remains disconnected. The frequency of the voltage generated by the RSC keeps changing, following the Power Train rpms evolution, so that said flux angular speed corresponds to the synchronism one. For this, the B2B controller usually has an input from a Generator shaft position sensor, which also allows accurately measuring the rotation speed.

![](<lib/B2B comienzo RSC alimentado subsincrono.jpg>)

4 - When the Power Train rpms reach their target for the existing wind, then the stator switch is closed. When this happens the air gap flux has two components: the one produced by the Rotor current (supplied by the RSC) and the one produced by the stator (black vector on the stator winding). Due to the tracking done by the B2B controller, both have the same frequency (angular speed), although not the same angle.

At that moment the power (not just voltage) supplied by the RSC to the Rotor evolves so that the Power Train mechanical power becomes transferred to the stator. This change is done smoothly, from 0 to the wind power. Said power does not reach the grid entirely, since as seen in the diagram part of it enters the GSC to feed the RSC through the DC Link.

![](<lib/B2B en produccion 7 ms.jpg>)

## Evolution of the magnetic fluxes in the Generator

![](<lib/Generador flujos.png>)

In steps 1 and 2 above there is no magnetic flux in the Generator, since in both cases the stator and the rotor are disconnected.

When the rotor is connected, in step 3, it can be noticed that the flux generated by the rotor voltage (green vector) rotates in the same direction as the vector indicating the mechanical rotor rotation (central gray circle). This occurs because at that moment the Power Train speed is below the Generator synchronism one (transferred to the gearbox low shaft, i.e. in a value consistent with the blade rpms). The real flux generated by the Rotor corresponds to the red vector inside the rotor winding (result of adding the angular speeds of the physical shaft and of the frequency of the signal generated by the RSC). The calculation of this frequency is done so that the angular speed of the rotor flux in the air gap is equal to the one of the flux that the stator will generate when it connects.

In step 4, in production, the air gap flux is due to the one generated by the stator (black vector) and the one generated by the rotor (red vector). It can be seen that there is an offset between them, so that the rotor one is more advanced than the stator one, as if it "pulled" it. Check that the difference between both is in the order of 3º. To do so, in the "Induction generator .." synoptic press the "Rotor flux" label (yellow) until the presentation is in "modulus and phase" (M); then press the angle value box until the indication is in degrees (D). Repeat the operation with "Stator flux" and calculate the difference between both values.

You can observe that said difference increases when more power must be "transferred", that is, with more wind: raise the wind to 9 m/s and recalculate the difference. Wait for the new power to stabilize.

**Angular difference between the rotor flux and the stator flux**:  
To calculate the numerical value of this difference you must use the values associated with the "yellow" labels called "Rotor flux" and "Stator flux". Click on them to change the format from "Real Part/Imaginary Part" to "Modulus/Angle". The angle can be read in "Radians" (R) or "Degrees" (D) by clicking on the value.
