# Why do the blades rotate?

The basic principles governing the forces that appear on the wind turbine blades are the same ones that allow airplanes to ascend. The blades rotate for the simple reason that they are mounted on a "device" (HUB) that has only one degree of freedom: rotating in a plane perpendicular to the Hub axis.

## What are these forces?

Let us suppose we have an airplane wing in a wind tunnel: a place where we can create a controlled air flow, so that there are no turbulences (i.e. "laminar flow"). Inside this wind tunnel the wing is placed (represented by its section, delimited by the red line), and we fix it well to the outside of the wind tunnel, so that it cannot move. We will assume that this fixing is done with an internal bar that crosses the blade along its longest axis, precisely in the circle shown in the following figure.

![](<lib/Bladeconfuerzas.png>)

The effect of the wind coming from the left is a compression of the laminar flow on the lower side of the blade and a depression on its upper side. The result is a set of forces applied along the blade surface: this set of forces is indicated by the small dotted arrows. Since the blade is a rigid body, the total effect of these forces appears as a single force at the fixing point: this force is indicated by the red vector. The magnitude and direction of this force depends on the blade shape, the incoming wind speed and the angle between the blade chord (gray line joining the two ends of the blade) and the wind direction. We can decompose this red vector at will: in this particular case, it is convenient to think of this red vector as the sum of two forces: one to go up, called *Flifting*, and another that goes to the right, named *Fdragging*.

These two forces explain why the airplane is kept hanging in the air (due to Flifting), and why it must be pushed to the left (to overcome Fdragging) so that the situation is maintained.

Now let us change the airplane wing for the blade of a wind turbine and place it in relation to its axis. The situation is similar, but inverted. See the following figure where the vision that an observer located at the tip of one of the blades and looking at the Hub axis would have is presented. The wind would be facing them and the blades would rotate to their left in a plane perpendicular to the screen and passing through the black vector.

![](<lib/WindNacelle Panel Hub con fuerzas.png>)

In this case, the wind incident on the blade edge is the "Total Wind" (red vector), a composition of the real wind (blue vector) and the rotational wind (black vector) that the blade edge "sees", because it is rotating in the plane perpendicular to the screen and passing through the line of the black vector. We are assuming that the rotation occurs "to the left" and due to that movement the blunter edge of the blade "cuts" the air at a speed (wind) that is not uniform along that edge: lower the closer to the Hub. In this brief explanation we will assume that the speed of this wind that interests us is the "average" along the entire edge.

The left representation is a way of solving the problem of representing the rotating blade, by making the change of proposing a "view from the blade itself", as if we were sitting at the end of the blade looking towards the rotation axis in the Hub. The black vector would correspond to the wind we would feel on the face due to that rotation.

The total force is represented by the green vector, and similarly to the wind tunnel and airplane wing case, we decompose it into two forces: one that pulls the blade along the only degree of freedom it has (the rotation plane).

The second force (light brown vector) goes in the direction of the nacelle axis and is counteracted by the Tower; that is, this is the force that tries to bend the Tower.

The previous graph refers to an "average" section of the blade. The rotation effect of the blades produces the effect of varying the real angle of attack of the wind. This is the reason why the blade profile twists along its axis, to compensate for this effect.
